# Chronological Discovery & Multi-Wavelength Timeline (5 BCE – 2026)

For original-language primary texts (`漢文`, `한문`, `العربية`, `فارسی`, `Latina`) and manuscript/print archive links, see [[multilingual-primary-records]].

* **5 BCE (Spring):** Han Dynasty court astronomers record a transient guest/broom star in *Hàn Shū* (`漢書·天文志`) visible for >70 days (evaluated in [[archeoastronomy/star-of-bethlehem-hypothesis|Star of Bethlehem Hypothesis]]).
* **964 CE (Shiraz):** ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī re-observes *Qīṭus* (`قيطس`, Cetus) for *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābita* (`كتاب صور الكواكب الثابتة`), documenting Ptolemaic stars and Bedouin asterisms while $\omicron$ Ceti lies in a faint phase.
* **1070 CE (Dec 25):** Northern Song (*Sòng Shǐ* `宋史`) and Korean (*Goryeosa* `高麗史`) imperial astronomers independently record a guest star (`客星`) in **`Tiānjūn` (`天囷`, Celestial Granary)** right beside $\omicron$ Ceti.
* **1437 CE (Samarkand):** Ulugh Beg compiles *Zīj-i Sulṭānī* (`زیجِ سلطانی`) from fresh observations of *Qīṭus* without a permanent bright star at $\omicron$ Ceti's coordinates.
* **1596 (Aug 3 Julian / Aug 13 Gregorian):** David Fabricius tracks a 3rd-magnitude star in Cetus rising to 2nd magnitude before vanishing in October; Korean *Chosŏn Wangjo Sillok* (`朝鮮王朝實錄`) astronomers log a guest star in the same season.
* **1603:** Johannes Bayer engraves the star at 4th magnitude on fol. 34v (*Cetus*) of *Uranometria* and assigns it the designation **Omicron ($\omicron$) Ceti**.
* **1609 (Feb 15):** David Fabricius re-observes $\omicron$ Ceti at maximum brightness, proving it had returned.
* **1638–1640:** Johann Phocylides Holwarda proves $\omicron$ Ceti vanishes and reappears cyclically (~11 months) in *Dissertatio Astronomica* (1640).
* **1662:** Johannes Hevelius publishes *Historiola Mirae Stellae* in Gdańsk, naming $\omicron$ Ceti **Mira** (*"the Wonderful"*).
* **1667:** Ismaël Bouillaud publishes *Ad Astronomos Monita Duo* in Paris, calculating Mira's mean pulsation period as **333 days**.
* **1923:** Alfred Harrison Joy discovers Mira's hot blue companion (**Mira B / VZ Ceti**) spectroscopically at Mount Wilson Observatory.
* **1995–1997:** **Hubble Space Telescope** directly separates Mira A and Mira B (~0.6") and images the luminous gaseous mass-transfer bridge between them ([[mira-binary-system]]).
* **2005 (Apr 28):** **Chandra X-Ray Observatory** releases the first X-ray image of Mira AB, revealing a soft X-ray flare on cool giant Mira A and accretion X-rays on Mira B ([[chandra-xray-data]]).
* **2007 (Aug 15):** NASA's **GALEX** space telescope discovers Mira's **13-light-year far-ultraviolet tail** and bow shock (`PIA09958`, Martin et al. 2007 *Nature*) ([[galex-uv-telemetry]]).
* **2010:** Sokoloski & Bildsten prove Mira B's **white dwarf** nature via rapid optical accretion flickering (`arXiv:1009.2509`).
* **2026 (Aug–Oct):** **ALMA Band 6** full-polarisation observations (Vlemmings et al. 2026, `arXiv:2608.23695`) image a **giant magnetized SiO-maser loop eruption** (~12 AU / ~8 $R_*$) launched from Mira A—demonstrating that CME-like magnetic eruptions can drive episodic mass loss on AGB stars ([[omicron-ceti-mechanics]]).
